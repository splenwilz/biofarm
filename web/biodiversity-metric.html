The Biodiversity Metric

Understanding what it means for your development.

The statutory biodiversity metric assesses biodiversity value using a range of ecological and spatial factors. While the methodology may evolve over time, its core purpose remains the same: to deliver measurable, lasting gains for nature.

What the assessment considers

Some of the factors considered in the assessment include:

Habitat type

The distinct habitat present on a site.

Habitat condition

The quality and ecological health of the habitat.

Habitat size

The area or length of habitat being assessed.

Strategic significance

Whether the habitat contributes to local nature recovery priorities.

Location

Where habitats are created or enhanced, and their relationship to existing ecological networks.

Time to target condition

How long it is expected to take a habitat to reach its intended ecological value.

Difficulty of creation or enhancement

The level of risk and complexity involved in delivering the habitat successfully.

What is the Biodiversity Metric?

The Biodiversity Metric is the standard approach used in England to measure biodiversity before and after development.

It compares the biodiversity value of existing habitats with the value that will remain or be created once a development is complete.

The difference determines whether Biodiversity Net Gain has been achieved and how many biodiversity units are required.

It is more than a calculation. It helps ensure the places we build today continue to support nature for generations to come.

What influences the Biodiversity Metric?

Every site is different. The Biodiversity Metric considers several factors when calculating biodiversity value.

Habitat type

Some habitats naturally support more biodiversity than others. Broadleaved woodland, species rich grassland and wetlands all carry different ecological value.

Habitat condition

Healthy habitats are worth more than degraded habitats. Protecting and restoring existing habitats can therefore play an important role in Biodiversity Net Gain.

Habitat size

The size of a habitat influences the biodiversity value it can support. Larger, connected habitats generally provide greater ecological benefits.

Strategic significance

Habitats that support local priorities, including Local Nature Recovery Strategies, may carry greater value within the Biodiversity Metric. This encourages biodiversity to be created where it will have the greatest impact.

Time

Habitats take time to establish. Some can be created relatively quickly. Others take decades to reach their target condition. The Biodiversity Metric reflects this through the Temporal Risk Multiplier.

Biofarm insight

The Biodiversity Metric is about more than numbers. It helps guide where habitats should be created, how they should be managed and how development can leave nature in a better state than before.

Why location matters

One of the most misunderstood parts of the Biodiversity Metric is location.

Biodiversity is generally more valuable when it is delivered close to the development it supports. The Biodiversity Metric recognises this through the Spatial Risk Multiplier.

Where biodiversity is created further away, additional biodiversity units may be required.

That is why Biofarm is building a growing network of habitat banks across England. Creating habitats closer to developments helps support planning, reduce unnecessary costs and strengthen local nature recovery.

What is the Spatial Risk Multiplier?

The Spatial Risk Multiplier encourages biodiversity to be delivered as close as possible to the development it supports.

Choosing a locally appropriate Biofarm Habitat Bank can help reduce additional biodiversity unit requirements while creating better outcomes for nature.

Who completes the Biodiversity Metric?

The Biodiversity Metric is normally completed by a suitably qualified ecologist. Biofarm works alongside ecologists, planning consultants and developers to help interpret the results and identify appropriate off site habitat options where required.

What is strategic significance?

Strategic significance recognises habitats that support local ecological priorities, including Local Nature Recovery Strategies. Creating habitats in these locations can contribute more effectively to wider nature recovery.

What is the Spatial Risk Multiplier?

The Spatial Risk Multiplier reflects the importance of creating biodiversity close to the development it supports. Choosing locally appropriate habitat banks can help reduce additional biodiversity unit requirements.

What is the Temporal Risk Multiplier?

The Temporal Risk Multiplier recognises that habitats take time to establish. Habitats that take longer to reach their target condition carry greater delivery risk, which is reflected within the Biodiversity Metric.

Does every development require the Biodiversity Metric?

Most developments subject to Biodiversity Net Gain will need the Biodiversity Metric to demonstrate how biodiversity has been assessed and how the required uplift will be achieved.

What happens if my site cannot achieve Biodiversity Net Gain?

Where sufficient Biodiversity Net Gain cannot be delivered on site, developers may need to secure off site biodiversity units from professionally managed habitat banks.
