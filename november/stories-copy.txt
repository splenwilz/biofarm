In conversation | Dan Massie, Development Director, The Hill Group (approved)

BNG from the developer’s side

For housebuilders, biodiversity net gain (BNG) is now part of the everyday reality of development. But what does delivering it actually look like, beyond the regulations and the metric?

We caught up with Dan Massie, Development Director at The Hill Group, one of the UK's leading privately owned housebuilders, to hear how BNG is playing out from the developer's side of the table. We talked about its place in Hill's wider business strategy, the decisions behind on-site and off-site delivery, lessons learnt along the way and what makes a good BNG partner.

Where does BNG sit within Hill’s wider business strategy?

For us, it's part of something much bigger than a planning requirement. Placemaking and social value are central to what we do at Hill. We're creating places where people will live for generations, so the environment around those homes really matters.

BNG has brought more structure to how we approach biodiversity, but the thinking behind it isn't new to us. We take responsibility for the long-term impact of what we build by creating sustainable places to live and contributing positively to local communities.

How has BNG changed the way you approach development?

I think the biggest thing is making sure we're considering it early enough.

BNG needs to be part of the conversation from land acquisition onwards, through feasibility, master-planning, and design. Understanding the requirements from the outset means you have more options and can make better- informed decisions.

It also involves people from across the business. Ecologists bring essential technical expertise, but our development, land, planning and design teams all have a role to play. Getting those conversations going early makes a real difference.

How do you decide between on-site and off-site BNG?

There's no one-size-fits-all answer. We consider both and weigh up what makes sense for each development.

On-site BNG can help create attractive, greener neighbourhoods, but we also need to balance the needs of residents with those of nature, while thinking about the long-term management and maintenance costs of different habitats.

Off-site BNG gives us another option. We can support habitat creation and restoration locally, with benefits that extend beyond the development boundary to the wider community.

Ultimately, there's a commercial decision to make. It's about balancing ecological outcomes, the needs of residents, and what makes sense for the development.

When you need an off-site solution, what matters most?

Location is our starting point. We want to understand where the biodiversity gains will be delivered and how they connect to the communities in which we're building. Keeping those benefits local is important to us.

Price matters, of course, but credibility and integrity are critical. Can the provider genuinely deliver what they're promising? Do they have the ecological expertise, financial security and long-term commitment to manage those habitats for the next 30 years?

With housebuilding activity subdued, the market for off-site BNG units is competitive, and we're seeing some keen pricing. It'll be interesting to see how that changes as the housing market recovers and demand picks up.

Whatever the market conditions, though, you need to look beyond the price tag. It's about finding the right location and a partner you can trust to deliver what they've committed to, not simply the cheapest.

What has surprised you about delivering BNG in practice?

Some of the things we thought might be challenging have actually been more straightforward than expected.

Brownfield developments are a good example. We initially anticipated that meeting BNG requirements on these sites could be difficult, but that hasn't necessarily been our experience.

The planning process itself can be slow, but BNG hasn't been the reason for those delays. We've never had a development stalled because of BNG, and it hasn't prevented us from progressing a land acquisition either.

That's not to say there aren't things to work through. Every development is different, but our experience has been that BNG is something you can plan for and deliver alongside the other considerations that come with development.

What has stood out to you about Biofarm’s approach?

Biofarm stood out against the criteria we look for in an off-site provider, particularly around location, ecological credibility and confidence in delivery.

There's a personal element too. Hill is a family-run and privately-owned business, as is Biofarm, and I think there's a shared sense of investment in what we do and the relationships we build.

What I've appreciated is their openness. They're always willing to have a conversation, explore an idea or offer a view, even when it might not lead to us working together on a particular development.

That makes a difference. Having people you can pick up the phone to, talk things through with and get a considered answer from is valuable.

What do you think comes next for BNG?

I think the next really interesting phase will be understanding what BNG looks like once habitats are under management.

We've spent a lot of time as an industry getting to grips with the regulations, the metrics, and how to deliver biodiversity gains. But nature takes time. We're not going to have all the answers after a year or two.

It will take time to build up meaningful data, understand how habitats are developing and see what the management approaches are actually achieving.

That's when we'll start to learn much more about what works, what doesn't and where we can improve.

Ultimately, the next chapter is about understanding what those commitments are delivering in practice, not just what was agreed at the beginning.


Lesnewth Habitat Bank

Habitat restoration progresses at Lesnewth Habitat Bank in Cornwall

The next phase of habitat creation is taking shape at Biofarm’s Lesnewth Habitat Bank in North Cornwall, with plans to restore species-rich grassland and establish new habitats for wildlife.

Spanning 26.8 hectares, Lesnewth is a registered habitat bank with 130 biodiversity units. Set within the River Valency landscape, the bank will help reconnect habitats around Cornwall’s rare Celtic rainforest, while providing off-site biodiversity net gain (BNG) units for development in the region.

Our team recently returned to Lesnewth to meet landowners Simon and Natsha and finalise plans for the next phase of habitat creation.

The focus is on restoring species-rich grassland and establishing new areas of native scrub. The first scrub planting is planned for later this year, with preparations underway for wildflower seed sowing next year alongside Simon’s local grazier.

Together with the site’s woodland, trees and Cornish hedgerows, these improvements will create a more varied, connected landscape with more food, shelter and space for wildlife.

Local partnerships, long-term restoration

Working closely with landowners and local people is central to how we create and manage our habitat banks. At Lesnewth, local knowledge and expertise are helping turn restoration plans into work on the ground.

“Biofarm brings together practical understanding of land and property with the ecological expertise to guide its restoration. They’ve made a complex process feel straightforward and given me confidence in what we can achieve together for the land and local wildlife.”

Simon, Landowner at Lesnewth

Lesnewth is part of Biofarm’s growing national network of registered habitat banks. Its biodiversity units are backed by ring-fenced funding for 30 years of habitat management and monitoring.

Our wider network supports developers and Nationally Significant Infrastructure Project (NSIP) applicants with off-site BNG, combining local habitat delivery with the scale and long-term management needed for larger projects.

With the next phase of work approaching, Lesnewth is another example of what long-term partnerships can achieve for nature and local landscapes.

Sleight Farm | Bird Survey

Nature is responding at Sleight Farm: bird surveys show habitat restoration in action

Independent bird surveys at Biofarm’s Sleight Farm Habitat Bank in Bath and North East Somerset have recorded 60 species to date, including 28 of conservation concern, showing how habitat restoration is supporting wildlife.

Set across 70 hectares in the Mendip Hills, Sleight Farm is Biofarm’s flagship habitat bank, where former grazing land is being restored into a mix of species-rich grassland, woodland, scrub, hedgerows and wetlands.

Since 2023, we’ve been restoring natural processes across the farm. Longhorn cattle, Exmoor ponies and Tamworth pigs help shape the landscape through conservation grazing, alongside carefully timed hay cuts and natural regeneration.

The latest independent bird surveys, carried out by TN Wildlife during winter 2025 and the 2026 breeding season, show how wildlife is responding.

The results speak for themselves

During the 2026 breeding season, 48 bird species were recorded, including six red-listed species of the highest conservation concern.

Bullfinch: Territories increased from five to nine, supported by berries, fruit and minimal vegetation cutting.
Blackcap: Territories increased from 12 to 22 as developing scrub provides more suitable habitat.
Chiffchaff: Territories rose from 24 to 27, with grassland and scrub offering suitable habitat.
Whitethroat: Two territories were recorded, compared with none in 2025, as birds made use of developing habitat around the farm’s boundaries.
Spotted flycatcher: Two possible territories were recorded for this red-listed species, supported by mature trees and insect-rich habitats.

Winter surveys also recorded around 200 redwings feeding on berries and fruit across the farm.

Learning from nature

Habitat restoration isn’t a fixed process. Regular ecological monitoring helps us see how wildlife is responding and adapt our management accordingly.

The latest surveys will inform the next phase of work, including retaining wider hedgerow margins, rotating grassland cutting, protecting developing woodland and scrub from grazing where appropriate, and exploring further wetland habitats.

It’s how we make informed decisions for the land and the wildlife it supports.

Delivering BNG that makes a difference

Sleight Farm has 374 registered Biodiversity Net Gain (BNG) units and is well placed to support development across Bath, Bristol and the wider West of England.

With units spanning lowland meadow, neutral grassland, woodland, scrub, hedgerows, trees and ditches, the site offers developers and ecologists a range of off-site BNG options to meet project requirements.

At Biofarm, our commitment goes beyond creating habitats. We manage, monitor and adapt them for at least 30 years, giving nature the time and care it needs to thrive.

BANES & Sleight Farm (awaiting approval)

A different kind of development, creating value beyond its boundary

At Englishcombe Lane in Bath, 16 new homes are being built with a very particular purpose.

The supported living development will give adults with learning disabilities and autism greater independence, with homes designed around the people who will live there and the support they need. Importantly, it will help more people stay within their own community, closer to family and existing support networks.

The development itself has been carefully considered too. Accessible and adaptable homes sit alongside shared spaces and a landscape designed to create a calm, sensory friendly environment. The approach has already gained national recognition, including Planning Permission of the Year (Outside London) at the 2025 Planning Awards.

Bath & North East Somerset Council is delivering the development in response to a clear demand for more specialist housing locally. When the project required an off-site solution for its biodiversity net gain, we worked directly with the council to secure the right units.

Keeping the investment local

Those units are being delivered just outside Bath at Sleight Farm, our habitat bank in Bath and North East Somerset.

Around 70 hectares of farmland are being managed for nature for the long term. Conservation grazing with Exmoor ponies is already underway, part of a wider plan to restore the land and create more space for nature.

For us, the local connection is an important part of this story. Investment generated by a development serving the local community is supporting nature recovery nearby, while creating long term value for the land on which that recovery will happen.

“For us, this was about finding the right solution for the development and for the area. Biofarm brought the ecological expertise and rigour we were looking for, with a clear commitment to delivering high quality habitat for the long term. Being able to do that locally at Sleight Farm made it a really good fit.”

[name], [job title], Bath & North East Somerset Council

Englishcombe Lane and Sleight Farm have very different purposes, but both are rooted in creating something of lasting value in Bath and North East Somerset.

One is creating much needed homes and greater independence for people. The other is creating and restoring habitats that will be managed for nature for at least the next 30 years.

A local development, supporting local people and local nature for the long term.
